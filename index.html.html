The problem

When the hardware becomes a commodity, the moat moves to service.

Radiometer built its name on analyzer performance. But blood-gas hardware is converging, and hospitals now weigh vendors on service contracts, uptime guarantees, IT and cybersecurity, analytics, AI decision support, and total cost of ownership. The brief: figure out what customers actually value, what they'll pay for, how Siemens, Werfen, and Abbott monetize service and digital - and where Radiometer's next recurring-revenue lines should come from.

The approach

Build the evidence base an executive decision can rest on.

Industry research
Blood-gas market dynamics, critical-care workflows, healthcare-AI adoption, and cybersecurity for connected medical devices - plus a deep read of Radiometer's own portfolio and digital ecosystem.
Competitive intelligence
Pulled apart Siemens, Werfen, and Abbott's service contracts, support models, analytics, and AI initiatives into comparative frameworks showing the feature gaps and the white space.
Customer-insight framework
Defined the research hypotheses, interview themes, and segmentation so customer feedback could be read against real market context - not in a vacuum.
Opportunity identification
Mapped current capabilities against emerging needs and competitor moves to surface the service gaps and monetization opportunities worth pursuing.
The recommendations

From findings to a growth strategy.

I translated the evidence into recommendations across four fronts: a service strategy (contract redesign, support-model optimization, service-tier architecture), a digital strategy (cybersecurity offerings, analytics products, predictive-maintenance programs), an AI strategy (clinical decision-support opportunities and roadmap), and a revenue strategy (new recurring streams, premium tiers, and pricing).

The thinking

"The real question for the C-suite: how does Radiometer evolve from an analyzer manufacturer into a diagnostics and healthcare-intelligence platform?"

Executive impact

A strategic growth initiative, not a research deck.

The recommendations fed directly into executive-level decisions - on multi-million-dollar service-revenue lines, product-roadmap investment, retention strategy, and where to place AI, analytics, and cybersecurity bets against Siemens, Abbott, and Werfen. That reframed the work from a market-research exercise into a growth strategy with enterprise-wide implications.

As an MBA intern on Intuit's Global Sourcing & Procurement analytics team, I rebuilt the Preferred Supplier Program - the engine meant to route 80%+ of Intuit's $7B third-party spend to the best-rated suppliers - replacing a brittle, manual data flow with an automated, closed-loop rating system wired straight into the data lake.

The brief

A program everyone relied on - running on a pipeline no one could maintain.

Intuit's Preferred Supplier Program exists to steer 80%+ of its $7B third-party spend toward the suppliers with the best price, quality, turnaround, and reliability. But the engine behind it was brittle: data was pulled from the data lake through Alteryx into Google Sheets, then hand-consolidated into a dashboard. Leadership knew it had to change - they just didn't yet have a picture of what "better" looked like.

Discovery

Interview first, architect second.

I started by mapping the real workflow - interviewing the Alteryx owner, sourcing managers, my manager, the director, and several VPs to learn where leverage leaked and what each group actually needed. Only then did my team and I design the target: one automated, closed loop to replace the manual chain.

What I built

Four moving parts, one continuous loop.

The dashboard
Pulls all 10,000 suppliers from the data lake into two views - an executive view of charts and a drill-down list across four taxonomy levels. The taxonomy shifted between datasets, so I built custom keys and complex joins to roll every metric cleanly up and down all four levels.
The ratings app
A JavaScript / Apps Script front end. Click a supplier in the dashboard and it opens a form - auto-populated and locked on the fields that matter for integrity, pre-filled with the last ratings, scoring preferred-vs-not in real time as a manager rates, and able to copy ratings across every category that supplier sits in.
The pipelines
Ratings save to Sheets, then Python / SQL pipelines push them back into the data lake - through a specific permissioned path I had to track down with IT - surfacing only the latest scores on the dashboard while preserving full history.
The employee loop
A Workato-driven Slack bot that, ~3 months after an employee uses a supplier's product or service, asks a one-tap "did it work?" - rolling those answers into a per-supplier employee score across all four levels and surfacing it on the manager's form. The preferred decision now reflects the people who used the supplier, not just the buyers.
The insight

"Compliance isn't a memo - it's a default. The leverage came from putting the right choice inside the decision, not next to it."

The result

Tested, shipped, and presented to leadership.

Everything but the Slack bot is tested and in production; the bot itself is built and technically ready, working through a change-management process I kicked off that ran past my internship. I presented the whole system at Intuit's campus fair and to the GSP leadership team.

  • → Maverick (off-program) spend fell 15% over the first year the tool was live.
  • → A single performance view across 10,000 suppliers and four taxonomy levels.
  • → Preferred-status scored live at the point of decision - review time cut ~60% for 30 sourcing managers.
  • → Supplier ratings automated into the data lake, with full history preserved for guided buying at scale.
  • → A two-sided score: sourcing-manager ratings and real employee feedback.

Workstream 1 · Vistas

Validating someone else's model - and finding where the optimism hid.

A cross-border media acquisition (Reliance Entertainment) at a ~$70M enterprise value. An external consultant had built the LBO; Vistas's chief strategy officer handed it to me because the deal looked too good to be true and he wanted to know why. My job was to validate someone else's model and find where the optimism was hiding.

It traced to three assumptions that compounded on each other:

Aggressive target revenue
The target's own revenue projections were steep. I pulled Reliance's actual financials to cross-check rather than take the model on faith.
Synergies stacked on top
Cost-synergy assumptions layered onto the combined entity made it more optimistic still - so the errors compounded.
A heroic exit multiple
A 15× exit off a ~7.2× entry assumed a lot of multiple expansion - optimism hiding in plain sight.

So I stress-tested three levers: sensitized revenue growth down toward what the actuals supported, haircut the synergies on the combined entity, and swept the exit multiple back toward entry. Each one pulled IRR down hard on its own; together they showed the base case was leaning on three optimistic assumptions at once. Once I flagged it, the firm brought in a second consultant and we ran sessions with the target to re-evaluate the synergies - landing on a defensible value.

Workstream 2 · Obsidian

Building the forward model for a media-receivables fund.

Obsidian Creek - the sister entity, newly launched when I joined - was raising a private-credit fund lending against film & TV receivables. I built the forward model projecting how the book scales from a $100M starting point to $500M+ over four years.

The spine was capital deployment and recycling: these are short-duration deals - 6 to 14 months - so the same capital turns over multiple times a year and the book compounds faster than the headline yield suggests. I modeled deployment pace, deal size and duration, gross yield net of expected losses, then layered the fee structure (2% / 20% over an 8% hurdle) to get to net investor IRR and the AUM path.

I also ran the market research and positioning for an OTT-financing push into Saudi Arabia, helping secure $25M in committed capital aligned with local content-development initiatives. The ~$1.7B addressable slice growing ~23% was the context - evidence the deal flow existed to deploy into.

Workstream 3 · Obsidian

The diligence engine: turning review into infrastructure.

Diligence was the bottleneck, so I designed and shipped an AI document-review tool for Obsidian's five-stage deal process. At each gate the AI reads the documents and returns a summary, the key figures, a timeline, and any SOP rule-deviations - with a citation on every finding so a human can verify it - and checks every name against a NO-GO list. Two-step sign-off (Ops, then Approvers) advances the deal.

Package→ Model→ Diligence→ Legal→ Closed

It surfaced 40+ SOP deviations across 300 documents with source-cited findings and cut analyst review time per deal by ~75% - built on a deliberately boring, hand-off-able stack: Next.js, PostgreSQL, and the Anthropic API.

"The best analysts don't just answer the question faster - they build the thing that answers it every time after."

The result

A deal de-risked, a fund narrative, and a faster engine - at once.

The bet

No contacts, no safety net - so I bought credibility with location.

In 2013 I turned down an IT offer to pursue sound engineering, and in 2015 teamed up with classmates, raised seed funding, and opened an audio studio in Mumbai - the Hollywood of India - with no industry contacts to lean on. The thesis was simple: out-compete on access and breadth. Offer every audio service under one roof, on a budget, from a serious address, and let the work earn the network we didn't have.

The grind

Door-to-door - as an introvert - until the breaks came.

Getting started meant cold calls and walking office to office pitching our services - deeply uncomfortable for someone naturally reserved. I took unpaid projects to build a reel, and after each rejection sat down to dissect what went wrong and sharpen the pitch. A digital pivot - Facebook plus a growing portfolio - finally landed a project with Prana Studios, a major animation house. That was the turning point that put us on the map.

The work

Blockbuster trailers, brand films, and campaigns with a point of view.

The studio cut music for blockbuster Bollywood trailers like Super 30 and URI, took on full feature scoring for JL 50 (SonyLIV), and produced for Amazon Prime, MTV and a wall of brands. We also worked across borders - an Etisalat UAE launch spot, an NGO documentary in Switzerland, projects in Turkey - and used the work to push on causes: a rap for the nonprofit Dasra spotlighting India's female athletes, and an Uber campaign promoting public transit during Delhi's pollution crisis.

The scale-up

A studio bet, then a system to keep it full.

In 2017 I made the call that changed the business: leasing a professional studio in a prime location despite the cost. I rebuilt the signal chain, upgraded to 5.1 surround, and brought on project-based talent - which let us deliver at a higher standard and charge for it. To keep the room full, I taught myself Google Ads and ran a tracked outreach pipeline that pushed the studio's owned channel past 1M+ views and converted that reach into recurring brand accounts.

"Owning a P&L teaches you what a spreadsheet can't: every assumption eventually has to pay rent."

What it taught me - and where it pointed

The creative work wins clients. Financial discipline keeps the doors open.

Running the whole P&L taught me the lesson the studio almost learned the hard way: what sustains a business isn't the creative output - it's the financial discipline behind it. I registered the company as an MSME to enforce payment terms and protect against defaults, managed cash and receivables by hand, and felt the cost of every loose assumption. When COVID dispersed the team, that realization is what pointed me here - to Python, the markets, the CFA, and finance.

  • → Full P&L ownership - pricing, costs, cash, and the consequences of all three.
  • → Built and ran a measurable, tracked go-to-market motion end to end.

The audit

Read the whole digital presence, then the market around it.

Cross-platform audit
Posting cadence, visual branding, engagement, and messaging across Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn, and X - distilled into a SWOT of the digital presence.
Influencer landscape
Mapped the clean-energy creator ecosystem into four tiers - thought leaders, podcasters, lifestyle creators, and adjacent educators - with audience sizes by platform.
Competitive benchmarking

Engagement benchmarks
Set realistic like-to-follower targets by audience size (≈3-8% under 100k followers), so "growth" had a measurable bar.
The insight

Lead with human impact, not kilowatts.
